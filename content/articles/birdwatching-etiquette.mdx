So, you’ve decided to take up birdwatching. Congratulations! You’ve entered a world where people get genuinely excited about spotting a warbler and will debate the merits of different binocular brands with the intensity of sports fans arguing over the greatest quarterback of all time. (Hint: It’s still up for debate.)

But before you grab your gear and rush into the great outdoors, let’s talk about birdwatching etiquette. Because, believe it or not, birds do gossip, and you don’t want to be the subject of avian side-eye.

## 1. Silence Is Golden (Unless You’re a Bird)

Birds have excellent hearing, and so do most birders. That means your excited shriek of “OH MY GOODNESS, A BLUE JAY!” will not only scare off the blue jay but also earn you a glare from every seasoned birder within a half-mile radius.

If you absolutely must express your excitement, do it in hushed tones—or better yet, a celebratory fist pump.

## 2. Blend In, Don’t Stand Out

Birds are naturally wary creatures, and your neon-orange hiking jacket might as well be a flashing “Danger! Human Approaching!” sign. Stick to muted colors, earth tones, and natural fabrics.

Think “woodland ninja,” not “fashion-forward hiker.”

## 3. The Binocular Rule: Look, Don’t Stare (at Other Birders)

Binoculars are for watching birds, not for people-watching. Yes, that person in the floppy hat and camo vest may be fascinating, but peering at them through your zoom lens like they’re an exotic species will only make things weird. Stick to feathered subjects.

## 4. Respect the Personal Space of Birds

If you’ve ever had someone stand way too close to you in a checkout line, you understand how a bird feels when an eager human gets up in its personal bubble.

Keep a respectful distance. Use those fancy binoculars and telephoto lenses instead of creeping up on a bird like a feathered celebrity stalker.

## 5. No Bait-and-Switch with Bird Calls

Using a bird call app or a whistle to lure birds out of hiding may seem like a great idea, but to them, it’s the equivalent of getting catfished. (Don’t be afraid to click that Wiki link for the explanation. The term was suggested to me for use here.)

Imagine responding to a call that sounds like your soulmate, only to find out it’s just some guy with a smartphone. Not cool.

## 6. Leave No Trace (Except Footprints, and Even Those Should Be Minimal)

Birdwatching should be a leave-no-trace activity. That means no littering, no disturbing nests, and definitely no pocketing of eggs or feathers as souvenirs.
Birds work hard on their homes. Don’t be the rude guest who trashes the place.

## 7. Be Nice to Other Birders

If someone is staring intensely into the trees, there’s a good chance they’re watching something amazing. Do not—repeat, do not—barrel in with a loud “Whatcha lookin’ at?” and scare away their once-in-a-lifetime sighting. A polite whisper or a subtle nod of inquiry will do just fine.

## 8. Keep Pets (and Kids) under Control

You may love dogs, but do birds? Not so much. If you must bring a pet, keep them on a leash and well-behaved.

The same applies to children. Unsupervised, both species have been known to chase birds, bark, and cause general mayhem.

## 9. Share the View

If you’ve spotted something incredible, don’t be a bird-hog. Let others have a look through your scope, point out where the bird is, and share your excitement. Birdwatching is a community activity, and few things are more rewarding than helping someone else see their first rare bird.

## 10. Enjoy the Experience

At the end of the day, birdwatching isn’t just about ticking species off a list. It’s about appreciating nature, slowing down, and learning from the birds themselves.

Be patient, be observant, and above all, have fun. And if the birds do end up gossiping about you, at least let it be for something interesting—like your impeccable bird-spotting skills, not your loud voice or questionable fashion choices.

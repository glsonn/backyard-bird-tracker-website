Putting out a bird feeder is easy.

Keeping it clean is the part that takes a little more effort.

And, honestly, it's not the most exciting part of backyard birding. Nobody sits on the patio with their coffee thinking, _I can't wait to scrub that feeder today_.

But clean feeders matter.

Birds spend a lot of time around feeders. When many birds are using the same small area, spilled seed, wet food, bird droppings, and old seed can accumulate surprisingly quickly.

A little routine maintenance can help keep your feeding station healthier for the birds and more pleasant for you.

The good news is that you don't need a complicated cleaning schedule or a cabinet full of specialized supplies. A few simple habits go a long way.

## Start With the Right Location

Before talking about cleaning, let's talk about where you put the feeder. Location affects both bird safety and how easy the feeder is to maintain.

Place feeders where you can easily reach them for refilling and cleaning. If you need a ladder, three people, and a minor engineering project every time the feeder needs attention, you're probably going to clean it less often.

A location with some nearby shrubs or trees can give birds a place to retreat if a predator appears. At the same time, you don't want the feeder so close to dense cover that a cat can sit hidden nearby and ambush visiting birds.

You also want to think about windows. Birds can collide with windows when startled or when reflections make the glass look like open habitat. Feeder placement can affect that risk, so don't assume that putting a feeder just anywhere near the house is automatically safe.

Your feeder should be convenient for you, reasonably sheltered for the birds, and positioned with nearby hazards in mind.

## Give Your Feeder a Regular Cleaning

This is the most important habit.

**Don't wait until the feeder looks disgusting.**

How often you need to clean it depends on the type of feeder, the weather, how many birds use it, and how quickly food disappears. A busy feeder in wet summer weather may need attention much more frequently than a lightly used feeder during a dry stretch in winter.

When you clean it:

1. Empty out the remaining food.
2. Take the feeder apart as much as practical.
3. Remove old seed and debris.
4. Wash it with hot, soapy water.
5. Rinse it thoroughly.
6. Allow it to dry completely before adding fresh food.

Follow the manufacturer's cleaning instructions if your feeder has special materials or components.

And don't forget the little places. Seed gets trapped in corners, seams, drainage holes, and beneath perches. Those are exactly the spots that are easy to overlook.

## Don't Add Fresh Seed on Top of Old Seed

This is an easy habit to fall into. You look at the feeder and see that there's still a little seed left. So you pour more on top.

Problem solved.

Except now the oldest seed is sitting at the bottom, possibly getting damp, moldy, or stale while the birds eat the newer seed from the top.

Instead, empty the feeder periodically and start fresh.

If there's a lot of perfectly good seed left, you don't necessarily have to throw it away. You can inspect it and store it properly if it's still dry and fresh. But if it smells musty, looks moldy, or has become wet and clumped together, don't feed it to the birds.

I’ve learned this is especially true of nyjer. Those tiny black seeds might look fine to you, but American Goldfinches will ignore food that looks(?), smells(?), feels(?) stale to them.

## Keep Seed Dry

Moisture is one of the biggest enemies of bird seed. Rain and snow can get into feeders, especially open platform feeders. Wet seed can spoil, mold, or become an attractive place for bacteria and fungi to grow.

Choose feeders with drainage and some protection from the weather when appropriate.

After a stretch of wet weather, take a look inside. If the seed is damp, don't simply stir it around and hope it dries. Clean out the feeder and replace the food.

This is another reason not to fill a feeder completely full every time. A smaller amount that gets eaten regularly is often better than a giant supply sitting there for weeks.

## Clean Up Under the Feeder, Too

The feeder isn't the only thing that needs attention.

Look underneath it. You'll probably find seed hulls. And spilled seed. And more seed. And occasionally something you can't quite identify.

Some ground-feeding birds will happily clean up what falls from above, but a large accumulation of food and droppings isn't ideal. Nobody wants all those sunflower seed hulls, so they can accumulate quickly.

Regularly rake or sweep the area beneath the feeder and remove accumulated debris.

This also gives you a chance to notice something else: **what birds are feeding on the ground**. Dark-eyed Juncos, Mourning Doves, sparrows, and other species may rarely visit the feeder itself but can become regular visitors underneath it.

Cleaning up doesn't mean you have to eliminate every seed from the ground. It simply means avoiding a large, concentrated buildup.

## Keep Different Feeders in Good Shape

Different types of feeders have different cleaning needs.

### Tube feeders

Pay particular attention to the bottom and the feeding ports. Seed dust and moisture can accumulate inside even when the outside looks perfectly clean.

### Hopper feeders

Check the corners and bottom of the seed chamber. Make sure moisture isn't collecting inside.

### Platform feeders

These are generally easy to clean, which is one of their advantages. But because they're open to the weather, they can also collect rain, snow, droppings, and debris.

### Suet feeders

Remove old suet before it becomes rancid or spoiled, especially during warm weather. Clean the cage regularly and replace damaged or contaminated food.

### Nectar feeders

Hummingbird feeders deserve special attention.

Sugar water can spoil quickly in warm weather, so don't treat nectar like dry birdseed. Check it frequently, clean the feeder regularly, and replace the nectar rather than simply topping it off.
If the nectar looks cloudy, has debris or bugs in it, or shows signs of mold or fermentation, it's time for a complete change and cleaning.

## Don't Forget the Birdbath

A birdbath is essentially another bird feeder, except the birds aren't eating from it. And it needs cleaning, too.

Birds bathe, drink, splash, and occasionally do things in the water that you'd rather not think about.

Change the water regularly and scrub the basin when it becomes dirty or develops buildup. Fresh, clean water is much more appealing to birds than a birdbath that has turned into a miniature backyard pond.

During hot weather, check it frequently because birds may use it more often and evaporation can be surprisingly quick.

## What About Disease?

You may occasionally hear warnings about birds spreading disease at feeders.

There's a reason for that concern: when large numbers of birds congregate around a concentrated food source, diseases can spread more easily between individuals.

That doesn't mean you need to stop feeding birds. It does mean that **clean feeders and sensible maintenance are important**.

If you notice sick or dead birds around your feeding station, it's reasonable to temporarily take down the feeders and clean the area thoroughly. Local wildlife agencies may also provide guidance if there is a known disease outbreak in your area.

You don't need to become an amateur wildlife epidemiologist. Just pay attention. If something doesn't look right, don't ignore it.

## Clean Hands Are Part of the Routine

Bird feeders aren't particularly dangerous objects, but you're handling something that birds regularly eat from and around.

Wash your hands after cleaning feeders, handling old seed, or working around accumulated bird droppings. It's a simple habit and a good one to have.

If you have pets, keep them away from piles of old or spoiled seed as well.

## Keep Seed Storage Clean, Too

You can clean your feeder every week and still have a problem if the seed you're putting into it has been sitting in a damp garage for six months.

Store birdseed somewhere cool, dry, and protected from rodents and insects. A tightly closed container is much better than an open bag sitting on the floor.

If you notice insects, mold, an unusual smell, or moisture in stored seed, don't feed it to the birds. Birdseed isn't wine. It doesn't improve with age.

## A Clean Feeder Doesn't Have to Be a Perfect Feeder

It's easy to turn bird feeding into a never-ending list of chores.

- Clean the feeder.
- Clean the birdbath.
- Sweep underneath.
- Check the seed.
- Watch the squirrels.
- Check the windows.
- Repeat.

But don't let the maintenance take over the reason you're doing all this in the first place. You're feeding birds because you enjoy having them around.

The goal isn't to create a laboratory-clean feeding station. It's to provide food and water responsibly while giving birds a reasonably clean and safe place to visit.

A few minutes of maintenance on a regular basis is usually much easier than trying to rescue a feeder that has been neglected for months.

## A Simple Backyard Bird-Feeder Routine

If you want to keep things simple, here's a good routine.

**Every time you refill a feeder:**

- Check for wet or spoiled seed.
- Make sure the feeder is functioning properly.
- Look for obvious dirt or buildup.

**Regularly:**

- Empty and clean the feeder.
- Clean up accumulated debris underneath.
- Check for damaged or deteriorating parts.
- Inspect the surrounding area for hazards.

**After unusually wet weather:**

- Check the seed for moisture.
- Clean out anything that has become damp or moldy.

**For nectar feeders:**

- Check the nectar frequently, especially in warm weather.
- Replace old nectar rather than continually topping it off.
- Clean the feeder thoroughly.

**If you see sick birds:**

- Take the situation seriously.
- Consider temporarily removing feeders.
- Clean the feeders and surrounding area.
- Check guidance from your local wildlife agency if illness appears widespread.

That's really about it. It might seem like a lot, but you don’t have to do all of it every day.

## The Birds Will Tell You a Lot

One of the nice things about maintaining a feeder is that the job can become part of watching the birds.

While you're refilling it, you might notice that the chickadees are suddenly bringing youngsters. You might discover that the goldfinches have returned. You might notice that the feeder you've been ignoring is actually the favorite spot for your nuthatches. And you might finally figure out why the seed disappears so quickly.

(It's probably squirrels.)

The feeder is a tool. The real reward is everything that happens around it.

Keep it clean. Keep it reasonably safe. Pay attention to what's happening. Then step back, pour yourself another cup of coffee, and see who visits.

**Notice. Record. Remember.**

If you’ve ever found yourself staring at a sparrow and wondering, “Is that a song sparrow or just a really confident house sparrow?”—then congratulations, you’re on the path to becoming a birder. And if you already knew the answer, well, you might be one of the seasoned veterans who can identify a warbler at 300 yards through thick fog. Either way, the Midwest is one of the best places to be a bird enthusiast, and there are plenty of birding groups and clubs ready to welcome you with open…uh, wings.

## Why the Midwest?

Sure, the coasts get all the glory when it comes to migration, but the Midwest is like the ultimate birdie rest stop. Think of it as the [Buc-ee’s](https://buc-ees.com/) of the avian world. (If you’ve never visited a Buc-ee’s, go find one now.)

With its mix of forests, wetlands, grasslands, and Great Lakes, the region offers some of the best birding spots in the country. Whether you’re chasing the elusive Kirtland’s warbler or just trying to find a woodpecker that isn’t a Downy, the Midwest has it all.

## Top Birding Groups and Clubs in the Midwest

_Note that each of the headings below is a link to that particular group. As of this writing, Indiana uses Facebook, as opposed to a dedicated site of their own, to promote their group._

### 1. [Chicago Ornithological Society](https://www.chicagobirder.org/) (Illinois)

If you love birds but also enjoy the energy of a big city, the Chicago Ornithological Society (COS) is your kind of club. These folks are serious about birds—so serious they even have an annual Big Day competition, where birders race against the clock to spot as many species as possible in 24 hours. They also offer birding field trips throughout the Chicago area, focusing on urban hotspots and natural preserves.

Membership is open to all, and joining gives you access to exclusive events, monthly newsletters, and a network of experienced birders who are always happy to help newcomers.

### 2. [Wisconsin Society for Ornithology](https://wsobirds.org/) (WSO)

This group has been around since 1939, which means they were identifying birds before birding was cool. WSO is the go-to organization for everything bird-related in Wisconsin, from field trips to bird conservation efforts. They also publish The Passenger Pigeon, a quarterly journal with updates, reports, and enough bird facts to impress even the nerdiest of bird nerds.

Membership grants access to special birding events, early registration for their annual convention, and participation in bird counts and surveys. They also host a popular spring convention that brings together birders from all over the state.

### 3. [Minnesota Ornithologists’ Union](https://moumn.org/) (MOU)

First of all, any organization that calls itself a “union” sounds pretty official. MOU has been documenting Minnesota birds since 1929, and their website is packed with migration maps, rare bird alerts, and a calendar of birding events.

Membership is open to anyone, and benefits include access to field trips, an annual symposium featuring expert speakers, and regular birding reports. They also offer a mentorship program for beginners looking to hone their bird identification skills with the help of experienced birders.

### 4. [Ohio Ornithological Society](https://ohiobirds.org/) (OOS)

Ohio is home to one of the biggest birding events in the country—[The Biggest Week in American Birding](https://www.biggestweekinamericanbirding.com/) (BWIAB). If you haven’t been, imagine thousands of birders descending on [Magee Marsh](https://mwbirds.wordpress.com/2025/01/24/best-locations-for-birdwatching-in-the-midwest/), whispering excitedly about Prothonotary Warblers while wearing the best hats you’ve ever seen. The Ohio Ornithological Society helps coordinate events like this and keeps Ohio’s birding community informed and entertained year-round.

Members receive access to exclusive field trips, educational programs, and networking opportunities with birding experts. The society also runs an active rare bird alert system to help members track down exciting finds.

### 5. [Indiana Audubon Society](https://www.facebook.com/IndianaAudubon/)

Audubon societies are always a solid choice for birders, and Indiana’s chapter is particularly active. They manage the Mary Gray Bird Sanctuary, offer birding workshops, and even have a birding trail that takes you through the best spots in the state. If you’ve never been on an organized birding trip, these folks will make sure you’re looking in the right direction (and not just staring at a particularly suspicious-looking leaf).

Membership benefits include priority registration for field trips, educational workshops, and exclusive access to conservation initiatives aimed at protecting Indiana’s bird populations.

### 6. [Missouri Birding Society](https://mobirds.org/) (MBS)

Formerly known as the Audubon Society of Missouri, this group has a long history of bird conservation and community-building. They run birding festivals, organize field trips, and have an active online community where members post sightings and ID challenges (because sometimes, that blurry photo of a warbler is not as helpful as you thought it would be).

Membership includes access to birding data, special field outings, and participation in conservation efforts across the state. The society also publishes a journal filled with birding insights and rare sighting reports.

### 7. [Iowa Ornithologists’ Union](https://iowabirds.org/) (IOU)

Yes, their acronym is IOU, which is hilarious because if you join, they actually give you plenty of birding resources instead of owing you anything. Iowa may not be the first place that comes to mind for birding, but with the Mississippi River flyway cutting through, it’s a migration hotspot. The IOU has been documenting Iowa’s birds since 1894. So if you’ve got a bird-related question, they probably have the answer.

Membership includes invitations to birding trips, access to their extensive birding database, and the opportunity to participate in annual meetings where rare bird reports and conservation strategies are discussed.

## What to Expect When You Join a Birding Club

If you’ve never joined a birding club before, don’t worry. There’s no secret handshake (unless you count the universal birder nod).

Here’s what you can expect:

- Field Trips: Usually early in the morning, because birds are apparently “morning people”.
- Monthly Meetings: A mix of presentations, ID workshops, and impassioned debates over whether that bird you saw was actually a rare vireo or just a very misleading goldfinch.
- Bird Counts: Whether it’s the [Christmas Bird Count](https://www.audubon.org/community-science/christmas-bird-count) (CBC) or the [Great Backyard Bird Count](https://www.birdcount.org/), these events help track bird populations and give you an excuse to stare out your window for hours.
- Networking: You’ll meet people who can tell a Cooper’s hawk from a Sharp-shinned hawk just by their vibes, which is honestly impressive.

## Final Thoughts: Just Join a Group Already!

Birding is fun on your own, but, if you’re the more social type, it’s even better with a group of fellow bird enthusiasts who won’t judge you for getting overly excited about a chickadee. Whether you’re a beginner or a seasoned expert, Midwest birding clubs have a spot for you.

So grab your binoculars, practice your “I just saw a lifer” happy dance, and find a group near you. And remember, when in doubt, it’s probably just a House Sparrow, but check _just in case_.
